# The Economics of Living Together

## What happens when two households become one?

**GreyScienx research note — version 0.2**  
**Evidence status:** Structured hypothesis and model; not yet data-tested or peer-reviewed.

## Abstract

Cohabitation is usually discussed as a relationship decision. It is also a form of economic consolidation. Two people who would otherwise maintain separate homes can share housing, utilities, appliances, household work, financial risk, and access to capital. They may therefore achieve a higher material standard of living without earning more or consuming twice as many resources.

That private gain has less obvious effects outside the household. Fewer dwellings are required for the same population. Demand for small rentals, furniture, appliances, and household subscriptions may fall. Saving, debt repayment, discretionary spending, and demand for larger homes may rise. Energy and material use per person may decline. Measured consumption and even short-run GDP can fall while actual welfare improves.

The gain is not automatic or necessarily shared equally. Cohabitation can create crowding, dependency, unpaid-work imbalances, lost benefits, reduced mobility, and substantial separation costs. A household can be richer while one person inside it becomes economically weaker. The correct question is therefore not whether cohabitation is “good for the economy,” but under what conditions household consolidation creates a net surplus, who receives it, and where the released resources go.

---

## 1. The thought experiment

Imagine two adults living separately. Each maintains a dwelling, pays connection charges, buys furniture and appliances, cooks, cleans, handles emergencies, and carries the risk of losing an income alone.

Now imagine that the same two adults move into one home. Their wages do not immediately change. Neither becomes more productive overnight. The country has the same population and the same number of workers. Yet something economically significant has happened: two systems of household overhead have become one.

This research calls that process **household consolidation**.

The clean starting comparison is deliberately narrow:

- two adults who would otherwise live alone;
- no children or additional relatives;
- unchanged first-period income and employment;
- the same broad standard of living;
- no monetary value assigned to love, companionship, or romantic satisfaction.

Excluding romantic benefits is important. It allows us to ask whether cohabitation produces an economic surplus even before the personal reasons for living together are considered. Later versions can add children, extended families, marriage, home ownership, and different household forms.

## 2. The cohabitation dividend

The immediate benefit is easy to see but easy to understate. It is often described as “splitting the rent.” Rent is only the largest visible part of a wider **cohabitation dividend**.

Some household costs are almost entirely shareable. An internet connection does not need to double when a second adult enters the home. A refrigerator, stove, washing machine, dining table, alarm system, and many pieces of furniture can serve two people. Other costs rise, but less than proportionally. A couple may require a larger dwelling, but usually not twice the floor area. Electricity, water, and food use increase, but connection charges, lighting, cooking, refrigeration, and bulk purchasing create savings per person.

Some costs remain almost fully personal: clothing, personal care, individual transport, medical expenses, and much discretionary consumption. The size of the dividend therefore depends on the composition of spending. Someone whose budget is dominated by rent and fixed utilities has more to share than someone whose budget is dominated by private goods.

The dividend also depends on behaviour. A couple may use the saving to rent a better home, eat out more often, buy higher-quality furniture, or move to a safer area. In that case, measured expenditure may not fall much, but the household receives more or better services for the same money. The economic gain appears as higher quality rather than cash saving.

Research on household consumption supports the existence of meaningful economies of scale, especially for non-assignable goods. One collective-household study found that members of couples effectively faced lower prices for jointly consumed goods than singles did. The exact magnitude is sensitive to the population and method, so it should motivate a South African estimate rather than be imported as one. [Household Composition and Preferences](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/roiw.12483)

## 3. More than lower expenses

### A second income is household insurance

A person living alone must absorb unemployment, illness, variable self-employment income, or an emergency from one balance sheet. A two-income household can spread that risk.

If one partner temporarily loses work, the other income may preserve the dwelling, keep accounts current, and prevent expensive borrowing. This does not eliminate the lost income, but it can prevent a temporary shock from becoming a financial crisis. The protection is strongest when the partners work in different industries or face different risks. It is weakest when both are exposed to the same employer, region, or recession.

Economic research describes this as spousal or household insurance: consumption can be protected through the other partner's earnings, joint saving, and changes in labour supply. [Wage Risk and Government and Spousal Insurance](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28294)

### Small monthly savings can become large balance-sheet differences

The cohabitation dividend can be consumed, saved, invested, or used to repay debt. These choices produce very different long-term outcomes.

If two people release R6,500 a month and save it, they accumulate R78,000 in the first year before investment returns. Over several years, the effect can become a house deposit, a larger emergency fund, reduced interest expense, or business capital. A household that could not independently cross a minimum deposit or collateral threshold may gain access to an asset that neither member could reach alone.

This is one reason the economic effect may be nonlinear. Cohabitation does not merely add a little disposable income. It can move a household across a threshold: from renting to owning, from uninsured to insured, from revolving debt to positive saving, or from being unable to finance training to being able to do so.

### Household production also consolidates

Running a home requires time as well as money. Shopping, cooking, cleaning, waiting for repairs, paying accounts, and maintaining a dwelling all contain fixed tasks. Preparing two portions does not always take twice the time required for one. One person can handle a delivery while the other is at work. Skills may also differ: one may cook efficiently while the other handles maintenance or administration.

This can release leisure or paid-work time. It can also do the opposite. If one partner becomes responsible for most unpaid work, the household may gain while that person loses leisure, earnings, experience, and bargaining power. Time must therefore be measured separately for each adult, not treated as a free household input.

## 4. The household is not one person

The greatest danger in analysing cohabitation is to calculate the combined bank balance and call the result welfare.

A household can save money while distributing the gain unequally. One partner may control the accounts, own the dwelling, retain personal savings, or decide how the dividend is spent. The other may perform more unpaid work, give up a conveniently located home, lengthen a commute, or become financially unable to leave.

The division of the gain depends on bargaining power:

- relative income and wealth;
- ownership of the home and durable assets;
- legal and contractual rights;
- access to personal accounts;
- each person's outside housing option;
- responsibility for unpaid work;
- social norms and the practical ability to leave.

This distinction matters for poverty measurement. Household income is often treated as though every member can access it equally. Evidence and measurement guidance warn that household aggregates can conceal material inequality within the home. [World Bank poverty-measurement guidance](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/156931468138883186/pdf/2980000182131497813.pdf)

The correct test is therefore twofold:

1. Did the household as a whole create a surplus?
2. Is each person at least as well off as they would have been living separately?

A positive answer to the first does not guarantee a positive answer to the second.

## 5. The costs hidden by “split the rent”

Cohabitation has entry, operating, and exit costs.

Entry costs include moving, deposits, contract cancellation, duplicated furniture, and adapting the dwelling. Operating costs include crowding, privacy loss, conflict, extra commuting, and compromises over location or consumption. A better job may be harder to accept if it would disrupt the other partner's work. A person may also lose a grant, rebate, or housing benefit when combined household resources are considered.

Exit can be especially expensive. A breakup may require a new deposit, furniture, transport, legal assistance, and an urgent return to a tight rental market. A partner who gave up an independent lease or reduced paid work may bear a disproportionate loss. The expected cost of exit should be included even when no separation occurs, just as an insurer prices the probability of a claim.

The relevant concept is therefore not gross monthly saving but the **expected net cohabitation dividend**:

> shared-cost savings + insurance + time savings + improved access to assets, minus fiscal penalties + dependency + crowding + conflict + moving and expected exit costs.

## 6. Who should gain the most?

The tempting claim is that lower-income households gain the most because fixed costs consume a larger share of their income. That may be true in proportional terms, but it is not guaranteed.

Lower- and middle-income renters may receive a large benefit when two adequate one-person dwellings become one adequate shared dwelling. However, people with very low incomes may already share housing with relatives, rent rooms rather than complete dwellings, live in informal structures, or occupy crowded homes. Their remaining costs may be less shareable, and adding another person may produce substantial crowding.

Higher-income households spend more in rand terms and own more durable goods that can be shared. They can also afford dwellings with sufficient space to preserve privacy. But a greater share of their spending may be personal and discretionary, reducing the proportional dividend.

The relationship between income and the cohabitation dividend is therefore likely to be nonlinear. It should be estimated across:

- income and employment status;
- renters and owners;
- formal, informal, and room-based accommodation;
- metropolitan and non-metropolitan areas;
- dwelling size and number of bedrooms;
- people already supporting relatives;
- similar-income and unequal-income couples.

The average is less interesting than the distribution.

## 7. When many households consolidate

One household moving together is a private event. Thousands doing so alter markets.

The most direct macroeconomic change is that the number of households falls while population remains unchanged. If two one-person households form one household, the economy requires one fewer occupied dwelling. Furniture, appliances, utility connections, and subscriptions are also no longer duplicated to the same extent.

This does not mean every affected industry simply contracts. The released income does not vanish. It moves.

Some households will save it. Some will repay debt. Some will rent a larger or better-located home. Others will spend more on restaurants, travel, education, childcare, entertainment, or financial products. Cohabitation changes the composition of demand before we know whether it changes total demand.

The direction of the macroeconomic effect depends on three questions:

1. How much expenditure is genuinely avoided?
2. How much of the released amount is respent, saved, or used to repay debt?
3. How quickly do construction, industries, prices, and employment adapt?

## 8. The welfare–GDP paradox

Suppose two people each rent a home for R8,000. They move into a R12,000 home together. They now receive adequate housing while spending R4,000 less each month.

Their material efficiency has improved. Yet rental expenditure has fallen by R4,000. If that money is saved or used to repay debt, current consumption falls too. Measured GDP can consequently decline in the short run even though the residents are financially safer.

This is not a contradiction. GDP measures market production, not the efficiency with which households turn market goods into welfare. It does not fully count avoided duplication, consumer surplus, unpaid household production, reduced financial anxiety, or the option value of a larger emergency fund.

If the R4,000 is spent on other goods, total consumption may be largely unchanged but its composition changes. If it becomes saving that finances productive domestic investment, later output may rise. If housing construction and appliance production contract without offsetting investment, output may remain lower.

The paper's central macroeconomic proposition is therefore deliberately restrained:

> Household consolidation can increase welfare without increasing GDP, and may initially reduce measured consumption. The eventual GDP effect depends on respending, investment, imports, prices, and industrial adjustment.

## 9. Housing and cities

Housing is likely to transmit the largest external effect.

More cohabitation should reduce the number of occupied units required for a given population. The first effects would be higher vacancy, slower rent growth, or changes in the types of units demanded. Small flats may lose demand while larger one- or two-bedroom homes gain it. In supply-constrained cities, even modest household consolidation could relieve pressure on rents. In areas with flexible construction, the larger long-run response may be fewer new units rather than dramatically lower prices.

The relationship also runs in reverse. Expensive housing, unemployment, and recessions can cause people to delay forming independent households or to combine households. Research on recessions and household formation finds that weaker economic conditions can reduce the rate at which new households form, partly through shared housing. [What Happens to Household Formation in a Recession?](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0094119013000284)

This creates an identification problem. If cohabitation rises during hard times, a simple comparison may falsely make cohabitation look economically harmful. The hardship may have caused the consolidation rather than resulted from it.

Urban form may also change. A population living in fewer dwellings requires less residential land, fewer service connections, and potentially shorter infrastructure networks. But consolidation can increase crowding if housing supply and incomes prevent households from choosing adequate space.

## 10. Industries that lose duplication and industries that gain the dividend

The first group exposed to consolidation supplies goods whose demand is tied to household count rather than population:

- small rental units;
- refrigerators, washing machines, stoves, and furniture;
- household internet and entertainment subscriptions;
- alarms, insurance, cleaning, and other per-household services;
- utility connection and basic charges.

Demand need not disappear. Appliances wear out, couples may upgrade, and larger homes require different furnishings. The relevant effect is a reduction in units required per person.

Potential recipients of the released spending include:

- larger or higher-quality housing;
- restaurants, recreation, and travel;
- education and professional training;
- childcare and family-related goods;
- saving, investment, and insurance products;
- debt reduction;
- business formation.

Whether domestic production gains depends on import content. Replacing an imported appliance with a locally supplied service has a different GDP and employment effect from replacing local construction with an imported holiday.

## 11. Work, entrepreneurship, and dependency

Cohabitation changes the amount of income a person needs to survive independently. That can affect labour decisions in opposite directions.

Risk sharing may allow a person to search longer for a suitable job, retrain, start a business, or reject exploitative work. Shared household work or childcare may make employment easier. These changes could improve long-run productivity.

But the second income may also reduce the immediate need for both adults to work. Combined means tests can raise effective marginal tax rates. Unequal household work can cause one partner—often but not exclusively a woman—to reduce paid employment. Relocation for one career can damage the other.

There is therefore no theoretically honest universal sign for the effect on labour supply. Total hours may rise, fall, or remain similar while being redistributed between partners. The research should measure job quality, earnings paths, unpaid work, and individual financial autonomy, not only whether someone is employed.

## 12. Government and municipal finance

Public finances can gain and lose simultaneously.

Fewer occupied dwellings may reduce the infrastructure and service cost required per resident. Housing assistance and emergency support could fall if households become more resilient. Greater saving and lower default could improve financial stability.

On the other hand, municipalities may receive fewer fixed service charges from fewer accounts. Lower residential construction and household-goods sales can reduce associated tax bases. Benefit rules based on combined income may withdraw support from a person who does not actually control the partner's resources.

The fiscal question is not merely whether government pays less. It is whether consolidation transfers risk from the public balance sheet to a household that can bear it fairly and safely.

## 13. Energy, materials, and the environment

Household consolidation should usually reduce direct residential resource use per person. Heating or lighting one home, running one refrigerator, and sharing appliances require less energy and material than maintaining two complete homes.

A United States study estimated a substantially lower carbon footprint for a representative person living with others than for the same person living alone. The numerical result should not be transferred directly to South Africa, but the mechanism is relevant. [The Carbon Implications of Declining Household Scale Economies](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S092180091500213X)

The full environmental effect must include **rebound**. Money saved on electricity, furniture, or construction may be spent on travel, meat, electronics, or other carbon-intensive consumption. The correct comparison includes both the direct resource saving and the emissions created by whatever replaces the avoided expenditure.

## 14. Why South Africa is a revealing case

South Africa combines conditions that make the cohabitation question unusually rich:

- high unemployment and unstable income;
- severe income and wealth inequality;
- expensive well-located formal housing;
- long commutes and spatial separation from employment;
- multigenerational and extended-family households;
- informal dwellings and backyard rentals;
- unequal access to reliable public services;
- strong differences between metropolitan and rural household structures.

Statistics South Africa estimated about 63.2 million people and 19.6 million households in 2024, implying approximately 3.23 people per household. The corresponding 2002 figures imply roughly 4.10 people per household. Household count has therefore grown much faster than population in the published series. [General Household Survey 2024](https://www.statssa.gov.za/publications/P0318/P03182024.pdf)

In 2023, 26.5% of South African households contained one person. That does not mean they are all available or suitable for cohabitation. Many are older people, migrants, students, workers living apart from families, or people who prefer to live alone. It does show that a large share of the housing system serves one-person households and therefore carries separate fixed costs. [The State of South African Households in 2023](https://www.statssa.gov.za/?p=17283)

South Africa also exposes an important distinction between **cohabitation** and **crowding**. Two adequately housed professionals combining flats are not economically equivalent to another adult entering an already crowded family home. A credible model must condition on the household that would exist otherwise.

## 15. Provisional findings

This armchair analysis supports nine provisional conclusions.

1. **Cohabitation usually creates a gross economic surplus** when it replaces two complete one-person households with one adequately sized shared household.

2. **Housing is likely to be the largest source of the direct dividend**, followed by utilities, connections, appliances, and household production.

3. **Risk pooling and threshold effects may be as important as monthly saving.** The ability to survive unemployment, avoid expensive debt, or reach a deposit can produce nonlinear gains.

4. **The surplus is not necessarily divided equally.** Control of money, unpaid work, ownership, and outside options determine individual outcomes.

5. **The proportional gain is unlikely to decline or rise smoothly with income.** Existing living arrangements, crowding, spending composition, and housing quality can produce a nonlinear pattern.

6. **Welfare can rise while measured consumption and short-run GDP fall.** Avoiding unnecessary duplication is economically valuable even when it reduces market transactions.

7. **Housing demand should respond more strongly than population-based models imply.** Household count, not population alone, determines the number of dwellings required.

8. **Labour-supply, GDP, and fiscal effects are theoretically ambiguous.** Their signs depend on behaviour and institutions rather than the existence of the direct dividend.

9. **Separation risk belongs inside the economic calculation.** A high monthly saving can be outweighed for one partner by dependency and a costly loss of housing after dissolution.

## 16. What could prove the argument wrong?

The core hypothesis would be weakened if careful South African evidence showed that, after holding housing quality constant:

- two-person households spend almost twice as much as comparable one-person households across supposedly shareable categories;
- moving, commuting, benefit withdrawal, and expected exit costs absorb most gross savings;
- consumption volatility does not fall when partner income shocks are imperfectly correlated;
- savings and debt outcomes do not improve around genuine consolidation events;
- household-level gains are routinely offset by crowding or large individual welfare losses;
- energy and material savings disappear after induced consumption is included;
- housing demand responds negligibly to household formation after population and income are controlled for.

The broader macro claims would also be wrong if released expenditure is immediately and completely redirected toward equally domestic, labour-intensive production, leaving aggregate demand and output unchanged. This is possible; it is an empirical outcome, not something the model should rule out.

## Conclusion

Cohabitation is a small-scale merger. Two people combine fixed costs, risks, time, and balance sheets. Like any merger, it can create efficiency while also redistributing power.

At the personal level, the likely result is a higher attainable standard of living, greater resilience, and faster asset accumulation. At the economy-wide level, the result is fewer dwellings and duplicated goods per person, a shift in consumption, and potentially lower material and energy requirements. None of these guarantees higher GDP, greater labour supply, or equal welfare inside the home.

The most defensible statement is therefore conditional:

> Household consolidation creates value when shared costs, risk pooling, time savings, and improved access to assets exceed the costs of crowding, dependency, fiscal penalties, lost mobility, and expected separation—and when the resulting surplus can be distributed so that both participants benefit.

The first empirical task is not to estimate one universal number. It is to estimate the distribution of the South African cohabitation dividend by income, housing arrangement, location, employment risk, and bargaining position.

# Addendum A: Mathematical and economic model

This addendum formalises the argument. It is not required to follow the main paper.

## A1. Direct consumption economies

For person \(i\in\{A,B\}\), category \(k\), and household state \(z\in\{S,C\}\), let \(e_k=p_kq_k\) be the expenditure required for one person living alone to obtain a reference service.

Two separate households spend

\[
E_k^S=2e_k.
\]

A two-person household spends

\[
E_k^C=e_k2^{\theta_k}\chi_k,
\qquad 0\leq\theta_k\leq1,
\]

where \(\theta_k\) measures rivalry and \(\chi_k\) captures quality or behavioural change. The category dividend is

\[
D_k=e_k\left(2-2^{\theta_k}\chi_k\right),
\]

and the gross direct dividend is

\[
D^{direct}=\sum_kD_k.
\]

At equal quality, the proportional saving is

\[
s_k=1-2^{\theta_k-1}.
\]

## A2. Disposable income, assets, and the fiscal wedge

Gross flow income is

\[
Y_t^g=w_{At}n_{At}+w_{Bt}n_{Bt}+y_t^{other}.
\]

Disposable income in state \(z\) is

\[
Y_t^d(z)=Y_t^g-T_t(z)+B_t(z).
\]

The consolidation-related fiscal wedge is

\[
\Omega_t=Y_t^d(C)-[Y_{At}^d(S)+Y_{Bt}^d(S)].
\]

Wealth evolves according to

\[
a_{t+1}=(1+r_t)a_t+Y_t^d(C)-E_t^C-c_{At}-c_{Bt}-M_t,
\]

subject to

\[
a_{t+1}\geq-\bar b_C(Y_A,Y_B,\text{collateral}).
\]

The cash dividend is allocated between additional consumption, saving, and debt repayment:

\[
\lambda_C+\lambda_S+\lambda_B=1.
\]

## A3. Risk pooling

Income-shock variance is

\[
\operatorname{Var}(Y_A+Y_B)=
\sigma_A^2+\sigma_B^2+2\rho_{AB}\sigma_A\sigma_B.
\]

If \(\sigma_A=\sigma_B=\sigma\), the standard deviation of average income relative to a single income is

\[
R_\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{1+\rho_{AB}}{2}}.
\]

Risk value is measured through certainty-equivalent consumption. With CRRA utility,

\[
u(c)=\frac{c^{1-\gamma}-1}{1-\gamma},
\]

\[
CE_i(z)=u^{-1}\!\left(\mathbb E[u(c_i(z))]\right),
\]

and

\[
D^{risk}=\sum_i[CE_i(C)-CE_i(S)].
\]

## A4. Time and household production

Each adult has

\[
1=n_i+\tau_i+\ell_i,
\]

where \(n_i\) is paid work, \(\tau_i\) household work, and \(\ell_i\) leisure.

Household services are

\[
G=A_G
\left[a_A\tau_A^{\rho_G}+a_B\tau_B^{\rho_G}\right]^{\gamma_G/\rho_G}
K_G^{\delta_G}.
\]

At shadow wage \(v_i\), the time dividend is

\[
D^{time}=\sum_iv_i(\tau_i^S-\tau_i^C)+V(G^C-G^S).
\]

## A5. Individual welfare and bargaining

Period utility is

\[
u_i=
\alpha_i\ln c_i+
\beta_i\ln h_i+
\gamma_i\ln G+
\eta_i\ln\ell_i-
\psi_iCrowding-
\phi_iConflict-
\zeta_iDependency.
\]

The collective household solves

\[
\max_{\mathcal A}\mu U_A(\mathcal A)+(1-\mu)U_B(\mathcal A)
\]

subject to its financial and time constraints and

\[
U_i(C)\geq\bar U_i(S),\qquad i\in\{A,B\}.
\]

The Pareto weight \(\mu\) is a reduced-form measure of bargaining power.

## A6. Separation risk

Let \(\delta\) be the per-period dissolution probability, \(K^{exit}\) the exit cost, \(M_0\) the setup cost, and \(\beta\) the discount factor. Expected value is

\[
NPV_C=-M_0+
\frac{\pi-\beta\delta K^{exit}}
{1-\beta(1-\delta)},
\]

where

\[
\pi=
D^{direct}+\Omega+D^{risk}+D^{time}+D^{capital}
-D^{crowding}-D^{conflict}-D^{dependency}.
\]

Cohabitation requires positive feasible gains for both individuals, not merely \(NPV_C>0\) in aggregate.

## A7. Macro aggregation

Let \(S\) be one-person households and \(X\) the number of new consolidating pairs. Then

\[
X=\frac{\varphi S}{2},
\qquad
\Delta H=-X,
\]

where \(\varphi\) is the share of residents in one-person households who pair with one another.

Aggregate direct resources released are

\[
\mathcal D=X\bar D^{direct}.
\]

If a single household consumes \(h_1\) housing services and a pair consumes \(h_2\),

\[
\Delta Q_H=X(h_2-2h_1).
\]

A local housing-price response is

\[
\Delta\ln P_H\approx
\frac{\Delta\ln D_H}{\varepsilon_S+|\varepsilon_D|}.
\]

The first-round consumption effect is

\[
\Delta C=-(\lambda_S+\lambda_B)\mathcal D.
\]

A transparent partial-equilibrium output decomposition is

\[
\Delta Y=
\Delta C_{domestic}+\Delta I_H+
\kappa_S\lambda_S\mathcal D+
\Delta G+
\Delta NX.
\]

It follows that

\[
\Delta W>0
\]

can coexist with

\[
\Delta C<0
\quad\text{and}\quad
\Delta GDP<0.
\]

## A8. Public finance and environment

Net public revenue from household state \(z\) is

\[
R_G(z)=T(z)-B(z)+F(z)-SC(z),
\]

so

\[
\Delta R_G=X[R_G(C)-R_G(S_A)-R_G(S_B)].
\]

If resource use scales as

\[
E(n)=E_1n^{\theta_E}\chi_E,
\]

then

\[
\Delta E=X[E(2)-2E(1)].
\]

Total emissions must include operational savings, avoided embodied emissions, and respending:

\[
\Delta CO_2^{total}=
\Delta CO_2^{oper}+
\Delta CO_2^{embodied}+
\Delta CO_2^{respending}.
\]

# Addendum B: Illustrative accounting example

This is a worked example, not an estimate.

| Monthly item | Living separately | Cohabiting | Released amount |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Housing | R16,000 | R12,000 | R4,000 |
| Utilities and internet | R4,600 | R3,100 | R1,500 |
| Food | R8,000 | R7,000 | R1,000 |
| Transport | R6,000 | R6,000 | R0 |
| Private consumption | R10,000 | R10,000 | R0 |
| **Total** | **R44,600** | **R38,100** | **R6,500** |

At R60,000 combined after-tax income, saving rises mechanically from R15,400 to R21,900 if the dividend is not respent. Net welfare still requires adjustments for quality, moving, fiscal treatment, unpaid work, crowding, bargaining, and expected separation.

Using the 2023 household count and one-person-household share only as a scale illustration:

\[
S\approx19.005\text{m}\times0.265=5.036\text{m}.
\]

If 10% paired with one another,

\[
X\approx251,800.
\]

At the hypothetical R6,500 monthly dividend,

\[
\mathcal D\approx R19.6\text{ billion annually}.
\]

This is not a forecast. It assumes pairability, applies one hypothetical budget to everyone, and ignores selection and price responses.

# Addendum C: Empirical route

## C1. Parameters to estimate

- Expenditure by category for comparable singles and couples.
- Category sharing parameters and quality adjustments.
- Moving, furnishing, commuting, and expected exit costs.
- Income-shock variances and correlation between partners.
- Saving, debt, and consumption responses.
- Paid work, household work, and leisure by person.
- Individual control of income and assets.
- Housing-demand and supply elasticities by locality.
- Domestic and import content of shifted consumption.
- Municipal connection revenue and service costs.
- Operational, embodied, and respending emissions.

## C2. Identification

Cohabitation is a choice, so a comparison of current couples and singles is not causal. A useful panel event study is

\[
y_{it}=\alpha_i+\lambda_t+
\sum_{k\neq-1}\beta_k\mathbf1[t-T_i=k]
+X_{it}'\gamma+\varepsilon_{it},
\]

where \(T_i\) is the move-in date. Pre-event coefficients test anticipation and pre-trends; post-event coefficients trace adjustment. A companion study around separation tests reversibility and exit costs.

The primary estimand should be the effect on people who actually consolidate:

\[
ATT_y=\mathbb E[y_i(C)-y_i(S)\mid C_i=1].
\]

## C3. First empirical deliverable

Estimate a distribution rather than a universal mean:

\[
F(D^{direct}\mid
\text{income, province, tenure, dwelling, employment, prior household}).
\]

Then add risk, time, bargaining, fiscal treatment, and separation to move from the direct dividend to individual net welfare.
