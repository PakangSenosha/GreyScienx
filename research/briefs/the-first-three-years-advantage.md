# The First Three Years Advantage

## Central question

How large is the lifetime financial advantage of deliberately keeping living and transport costs low during the first one, two, or three years of full-time employment?

## Study scope

The study should examine:

- Moving out immediately versus remaining in the family home for one, two, or three years
- Salary at first employment and annual salary growth
- Contributions to parents or the household while living at home
- Rent, deposits, moving costs, utilities, food, furniture, and appliance costs avoided or delayed
- Lifestyle inflation after first employment
- Savings rates during the first three working years
- Emergency-fund accumulation and debt or student-debt repayment
- Building a property deposit and purchasing a first property earlier
- Investing early savings instead of consuming them
- Retirement contributions and compound investment growth over 10, 20, 30, and 40 years
- The opportunity cost of leaving home earlier
- Whether saving R100,000 at age 23 is more valuable than saving R100,000 at age 33
- Sensitivity to investment returns and inflation-adjusted outcomes
- Lower-, middle-, and higher-income graduate scenarios
- Johannesburg, Cape Town, and Durban living-cost scenarios
- Household wealth versus individual independence
- The financial value of parental support and contributions to parents as an alternative to market rent
- Psychological and social costs of remaining at home
- Independence, privacy, relationships, and longer commuting distances
- Whether remaining home becomes counterproductive beyond a threshold
- Income thresholds at which moving out early has little long-run financial penalty
- Whether the first three working years are an unusually important wealth-building window

## Transport sub-model: Uber First, Car Later

### Central question

Can ride-hailing or public transport, combined with delaying car ownership for one, two, or three years, produce a meaningful lifetime wealth advantage?

The transport model should include:

- Buying a car immediately after employment versus delaying by one, two, or three years
- Uber, public transport, and hybrid transport strategies
- Work-from-home days
- Vehicle purchase price, deposit, instalments, and finance interest
- Insurance, fuel, maintenance, tyres, licensing, parking, depreciation, repairs, and accident excess
- The opportunity cost of capital tied up in a vehicle
- Cash purchase versus vehicle finance and new versus used vehicles
- Saving toward a future cash purchase or a cheaper vehicle
- Investing the avoided car payment
- Distance-to-work and kilometres-per-month break-even thresholds
- Shift work, safety, surge pricing, weekend usage, waiting time, and convenience
- Access to a family vehicle
- Employment opportunities enabled by car ownership
- Using the vehicle for ride-hailing or delivery income

## Combined strategies

Compare at least these strategies:

| Strategy | Move out | Buy car |
|---|---:|---:|
| Immediate independence | Year 0 | Year 0 |
| One-year delay | Year 1 | Year 1 |
| Two-year delay | Year 2 | Year 2 |
| Three-year delay | Year 3 | Year 3 |
| Home plus earlier car | Year 3 | Year 1 |
| Home plus delayed car | Year 3 | Year 3 |

Follow each representative person for approximately 40 years and report wealth at ages 30, 40, and 60.

Do not assume that all accumulated capital enters the stock market. Compare alternative uses:

- Investment assets
- A housing deposit that changes later rent and home equity
- A cash vehicle purchase that avoids finance interest
- Education or training that may raise future earnings
- Debt repayment and emergency liquidity

Stress-test salary, rent, household contributions, transport distance, work-from-home frequency, vehicle prices, investment returns, and property prices. Identify break-even conditions where delaying independence or car ownership no longer makes financial sense.

## Main output

Calculate the lifetime wealth difference created by postponing full household independence and/or car ownership for the first one, two, or three years of employment. Identify the break-even thresholds and determine whether the first few years of employment represent a disproportionately important period for lifetime wealth accumulation.

The main comparison matrix should include:

| Strategy | Move out | Buy car | Wealth at 30 | Wealth at 40 | Wealth at 60 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Immediate independence | Year 0 | Year 0 | — | — | — |
| One-year delay | Year 1 | Year 1 | — | — | — |
| Two-year delay | Year 2 | Year 2 | — | — | — |
| Three-year delay | Year 3 | Year 3 | — | — | — |
| Home plus earlier car | Year 3 | Year 1 | — | — | — |
| Home plus delayed car | Year 3 | Year 3 | — | — | — |

## Editorial framing

Keep the research armchair, readable, and thorough. Put mathematics in the model implementation and explain its economic meaning in prose rather than filling the paper with equations. Treat independence, privacy, safety, family obligations, and relationships as real welfare considerations instead of implying that the financially richest strategy is automatically the best life strategy.

Headline question:

> **How much can three boring years in your twenties change the next forty?**
