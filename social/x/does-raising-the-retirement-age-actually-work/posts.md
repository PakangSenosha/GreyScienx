# Does Raising the Retirement Age Actually Work? — X campaign

## Post 01 — Flagship

Raising the retirement age works—if “works” means improving the public ledger. In this model, the social answer is harder.

The experiment follows one million people from age 60 and tests retirement at 65, 70 and 75. In the average labour-market scenario, the cumulative public gain rises from R213 billion at 65 to R369 billion at 70 and R448 billion at 75, all versus retirement at 60.

But the return to each extra five years shrinks: R213bn, then R156bn, then R79bn. Older-worker tax revenue flattens as retention falls, while disability exposure and unsupported bridge years rise.

At 75, the model produces 1.61 million disability claim-years and 3.99 million unsupported bridge-years. That is not a forecast of destitution. It is a warning that a legal age does not create a suitable job, restore health or replace support.

The youth result is not fixed either. At age 70, adapted jobs and expanding demand add 0.32 million youth job-years; the average case removes 0.79 million; stagnation and close job competition remove 2.16 million. The assumption about labour demand changes the answer.

So the policy sequence matters: jobs and health first, occupational flexibility and disability protection next, the age change last. Age 65 is fiscally robust across all three modeled cases. Moving to 70 can work when job design is explicit. Moving to 75 needs a much higher evidence threshold.

These are cumulative, undiscounted scenario results in constant 2026 rand—not a forecast or budget saving ready to book.
