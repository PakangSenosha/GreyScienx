## Post 01 — Flagship

Ten years of duplicated housing costs can still be visible half a century later.

In a GreyScienx lifetime model, two otherwise identical Gauteng couples each bring home R50,000 a month: one partner R30,000, the other R20,000. Both stay together from 25 to 75, raise the same children and, where the scenario allows, buy the same home. The difference: one couple combines households at 25; the other keeps two homes until 35.

Under the central assumptions, sharing one home releases R8,500 a month at 25. By 35, the Early household has R2.260m in combined net worth, including pension saving, versus R1.072m for the Later household — a R1.188m head start.

From 35 onward, their housing arrangement and modeled shocks are identical. But their balance sheets are not. The earlier saving keeps compounding, while a smaller buffer leaves the Later household more exposed to expensive negative balances.

At 75, the model puts combined household wealth at R13.833m for the Early couple and R9.861m for the Later couple: a R3.972m gap in constant 2026 rand.

The result survives across three scenarios. The age-75 advantage ranges from about R1.9m in the adverse case to R11.3m in the favourable case. That range is not a forecast. It is a counterfactual under stated assumptions, including that available surplus is invested.

Nor is this a claim that people should move in together for money, or that each partner owns half. Separate homes can buy privacy, independence, safety and time to test compatibility. Relationship costs and bargaining power can erase or reverse the financial gain.

The narrower finding is harder to dismiss: household formation has a balance-sheet effect. The direct saving matters; the larger force is time — decades of compounding and protection from deficits.

## Post 04 — A buffer, not a cure

Worst-case model: both households end with funding shortfalls. Moving in at 25 leaves the age-75 shortfall R1.882m smaller, but does not fix retirement. A buffer, not a cure.

## Post 06 — Household wealth is not equal ownership

Combined household wealth is not a 50/50 ownership statement. The model tracks one balance sheet—not who owns the assets, controls the money or performs unpaid work.

## Post 08 — The welfare–GDP paradox

One rent avoided can improve household finances while measured consumption falls. GDP does not count an avoided transaction as output. That is the welfare–GDP paradox.
https://greyscienx.com/research/household-economics/greyscienx-economics-of-living-together.pdf

## Post 09 — Household formation is housing demand

Housing demand depends on household formation, not population alone. For ten years, the model’s Later couple supports two homes while the Early couple supports one.
https://greyscienx.com/research/household-economics/greyscienx-economics-of-living-together.pdf

## Post 10 — When the result reverses

The modelled advantage can reverse if sharing barely cuts costs, the saving is spent, career costs are large, or separation costs exceed the benefit.
https://greyscienx.com/research/household-economics/greyscienx-economics-of-living-together.pdf
